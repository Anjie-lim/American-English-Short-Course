Module 1

American English Grammar & Syntax

Recognize the grammar and sentence patterns used in American English, with particular attention to the conventions covered in this training.

Why this matters for annotation

Grammar errors can change tense, agreement, or the relationship between parts of a sentence. Your task is to recognize the American English form required by the task, rather than simply deciding whether another form could also work.

Pay particular attention to tense choice, time prepositions, collective nouns, and American verb forms.

Start With Sentence Structure

Identify the subject, verb, and time or context of the action. Then check whether the form matches the intended meaning.

Completed action

We finished the review yesterday.

Present relevance

We have finished the review.

Review habit: Do not change a sentence simply because another form exists. First identify the convention the task requires.

Other Grammar Checks

Subject–verb agreement

The verb should agree with the grammatical subject: The report is ready. / The reports are ready.

Complete sentences

A complete sentence needs a subject and a verb that form a complete thought. A fragment such as “After the meeting” does not.

Run-on sentences

A long sentence is not automatically a run-on. A run-on occurs when complete clauses are improperly connected.

Past Simple and Present Perfect

The Past Simple describes a completed action, often with a definite or understood time reference.

Past Simple

I finished the report yesterday.

She called the client this morning.

Present Perfect

I have finished the report.

She has called the client.

Project convention: The reference notes that American English commonly prefers the Past Simple in some contexts involving recently completed actions.

Collective Nouns

Words such as team, company, committee, family, staff, and government refer to groups.

The marketing team is reviewing the campaign.

Got and Gotten

Got is the past form. Gotten is used as a past participle in the American construction taught in this module.

He got more confident.

Why vocabulary choice matters

American and British English can use different words for the same object or concept. For annotation, the key distinction is often regional vocabulary, not whether one term is inherently correct.

Identify the object or concept first, then select the American English term.

Module 4

American English Punctuation & Formatting

Apply quotation, date, and title-formatting conventions used in American English.

Why formatting matters

Punctuation shows where quotations end, clarifies sentence structure, and makes dates and titles easier to interpret.

Direct Quote Checklist

  1. Use double quotation marks.
  2. Capitalize a complete quoted sentence.
  3. Use a comma to introduce a quotation after an appropriate introductory clause or dialogue tag.
  4. Check where the ending punctuation belongs.
Maria said, “The project is complete.”
“The project is complete,” Maria said.

Different Punctuation, Different Rules

For the convention taught here, periods and commas go inside quotation marks. The reference also notes that colons and semicolons go outside, while question marks and exclamation points depend on whether they belong to the quote or the surrounding sentence.

SituationExample
Question belongs to quoteShe asked, “Are you ready?”
Question belongs to whole sentenceWho said, “Give me liberty or give me death”?
Colon outsideHe called it “unexpected”:

Dates and Titles

Numeric American dates follow Month/Day/Year. In titles, capitalize major words while keeping shorter articles and prepositions lowercase unless they begin the title.

Quotation Marks

American English typically uses double quotation marks for direct quotations.

She said, “The meeting starts at nine.”

Periods and Commas With Quotation Marks

In the American English convention taught here, periods and commas go inside quotation marks.

Correct

She said, “I'll call you later.”

Correct

He described it as “unexpected.”

Remember: Check the placement of periods and commas relative to the closing quotation mark.

Dates

American English commonly uses the Month/Day/Year order in numeric dates.

09/23/2024

Keep the format consistent within the same piece of communication.

Title Capitalization

Capitalize major words in a title. Articles and prepositions are generally lowercase unless they begin the title.

American Cultural Context & Awareness

Recognize common American cultural references, holidays, idiomatic expressions, and everyday social conventions.

Why cultural context matters

Words and expressions are not always interpreted literally. Cultural awareness helps you recognize references that are familiar within American contexts and understand how they function in everyday communication.

Literal Meaning vs. Cultural Meaning

Literal

“Spill the beans” could describe dropping beans.

Contextual

In common usage, it means to reveal a secret.

Holiday References

Independence Day

July 4 is associated with Independence Day and commonly with patriotic displays, fireworks, and outdoor gatherings.

Halloween

Halloween is observed on October 31.

Social Questions and Their Function

Short questions can function as conversational greetings rather than requests for detailed information. The intended response depends on the setting.

Context clue: Ask what the speaker is trying to accomplish socially. A greeting may function as a greeting even when its literal wording appears to request information.

Sports as Cultural References

American English content may refer to basketball, baseball, and American football, as well as major events such as the World Series or NBA Finals.

The reference emphasizes clear, concise communication and directness in American business contexts.

A brief question such as “How's it going?” may function as a polite social greeting rather than an invitation to provide a detailed account.

Module 6

American Idioms & Slang

Interpret nonliteral expressions and informal language by using context rather than taking every phrase literally.

Why idiom recognition matters

Idioms and slang can cause errors when a phrase is interpreted word by word. In annotation, identify the intended meaning supported by context.

Common Idiom Types

Action-oriented

break the ice

beat the clock

call it a day

State-oriented

on the fence

tough nut to crack

Use the Surrounding Sentence

“We only have five minutes left. Let's beat the clock and finish the report.”

The phrase refers to completing the task before time runs out.

Idioms vs. Slang

An idiom has an established nonliteral meaning. Slang is informal vocabulary or phrasing associated with particular social contexts. They can overlap, but they are not identical categories.

When Direct Language Is Better

Clear

The task is difficult.

Idiomatic

It's a tough nut to crack.

Best practice: Learn to recognize idioms even when you should avoid introducing them in your own annotation writing.
Annotation principle: Idioms can be useful for understanding meaning, but avoid unnecessary idiomatic language when writing AI-training responses because literal interpretation can cause confusion.

What Is an Idiom?

An idiom is a group of words whose intended meaning cannot always be determined by interpreting each word literally.

“Let's break the ice.”

This normally means starting a conversation or helping people become more comfortable, not physically breaking ice.

Common Expressions

ExpressionIntended meaning
break the iceInitiate conversation or reduce social tension
tough nut to crackA difficult problem or person to understand
beat the clockFinish before time runs out
on the fenceUndecided
call it a dayStop working for the day

Slang and Informal Language

Slang is informal language associated with particular social or conversational contexts.

Cool can be used informally to mean something is great, impressive, or acceptable depending on context.

“That's a cool idea.”

Use Context Clues

“The deadline is in ten minutes, but we still have three pages to finish. We need to beat the clock.”

Meaning: They need to finish before the available time runs out.

Literal vs. Intended Meaning

Literal reading

“Call it a day” sounds like naming a day.

Contextual reading

In workplace conversation, it can mean stopping work for the day.

When Writing for AI Training

Direct language is often easier to interpret than excessive idiomatic language.

Direct

Stop working for today.

Idiomatic

Let's call it a day.

A coworker says, “This software bug is a tough nut to crack.” What does the expression mean?
A trainer says, “We need to beat the clock.” What does this mean?
In informal American English, someone describes a new movie as “cool.” What does “cool” most likely mean?

Interpretation Strategy

  1. Identify the unusual expression.
  2. Ask whether the literal meaning makes sense in context.
  3. Look for surrounding clues.
  4. Determine the intended meaning.
  5. Use direct language when writing an explanation unless the idiom itself is relevant.

Final review

You should now be able to recognize common American idioms and informal expressions, distinguish literal from intended meaning, and use context to determine what a speaker or writer is communicating.
